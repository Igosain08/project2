Assignment 4 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Ishaan Gosain — igosain@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Abortion restrictions don't actually reduce abortion rates, but instead force women to travel to other states for care

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Score: +1.5 — I included a shaded 95% confidence interval behind the linear trend. While it appears thin due to low residual variance, it is statistically accurate and interactive (users can zoom in). This choice helps viewers see uncertainty without distortion.
  • Score: +2 — I added both a linear trend line and a quadratic fit with R² and r values displayed in a clear annotation box. This dual-fit choice presents the possibility of nonlinear structure while maintaining transparency about model strength.
  • Score: +1 — I scaled the bubble sizes to represent abortion rates per 1,000 women, using a minimum threshold to prevent visual exclusion of low-rate states. This ensured visual balance while still reflecting real differences.
  • Score: 0.5 — I labeled only a select few states (e.g., Missouri, South Dakota, Massachusetts) to emphasize outliers while avoiding overwhelming the chart. It guides interpretation slightly but is still fair.
  • Score: +2 — I transformed the raw data into three key metrics and chose meaningful encodings for each (x = access, y = travel, size = rate). The visual encodings reinforce the message while staying faithful to the data structure.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Score: +1 — I grouped states into 4 access categories based on an access_score (100 – % without access). This binning clarified the trend while preserving the natural distribution of state counts.
  • Score: +1.5 — I annotated the chart with the text “Policy Success” and highlighted the 76% difference in abortion rate between very low and high access states. This persuasive framing draws focus but is grounded in real differences.
  • Score: +2 — I started the y-axis at 0 and labeled all bars with numeric rates and state counts. These choices ensure clear, honest reading of magnitudes and reduce room for exaggeration.
  • Score: 0 — I did not label individual states to avoid regional bias or finger-pointing. This maintains neutrality and simplicity, though it hides variability within each access group.
  • Score: -0.5 — I used in-state abortion rate (not residence-based), which may understate demand in restrictive states. I noted this in the caption to partially offset the bias, but the decision remains mildly slanted.

Final Reflection

Designing these visualizations made me realize how powerful design decisions are in shaping a viewer’s interpretation — even when the underlying data stays the same. For the first chart (in favor of the proposition), I wanted to present a multifaceted argument, so I used layered encodings and added both linear and quadratic trend lines with their statistical metrics (correlation and R²). It was important to be honest while persuasive, so I included a true confidence interval and allowed interactivity to explore the details, rather than artificially widening it for effect.

For the opposing chart, I leaned into simplicity and clarity. The use of categorical bins, embedded labels, and the “Policy Success” callout were all meant to make the takeaway fast and bold — though I also knew that omitting cross-state travel rates was a form of omission bias. Still, the goal was to reflect how persuasive visualizations in media often work: they highlight truths but through selective focus. By calling out the in-state rate caveat in the caption, I aimed to maintain ethical boundaries.

This project helped me define ethical visualization as a balance between clear storytelling and honest representation. It’s not inherently wrong to persuade — but it becomes misleading when visuals obscure key limitations or suppress counter-evidence. I learned that transparency (like showing metrics, clarifying assumptions, and providing contextual captions) can make even persuasive charts ethically sound. The line between persuasive and deceptive design often lies in what’s left unsaid — and whether the audience has enough to draw their own informed conclusions.
